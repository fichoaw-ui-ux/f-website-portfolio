<PartHeader number="01" title="Project Snapshot" />

<Section title="Background">
Jack Finance is a business financial services provider offering integrated financial management solutions. One of its main services is ***Local Transfer***, built to help companies manage domestic payments in one place.

Since its main audience is corporate clients with high-volume transaction needs, **Local Transfer was designed from day one to handle bulk payments (*batch processing*)**.
</Section>

<PartHeader number="02" title="Context & Problem" />

<Section title="A. Context Problem">
Fira (my PM) came to me with one data point:

> **72% of batch transfers on Jack Portal only had one recipient.**

That 72% wasn't because people chose batch — it was because there was no other option. Every time someone wanted to send to just one person, they still had to go through a flow built for hundreds of recipients: name the batch, pick an upload method, work through a validation table. For a single transaction.

When Fira and I dug deeper, the problem wasn't just one thing. There were three friction points stacked in a row in the same flow — each one slowing users down before they even got to the transfer itself.
</Section>

<Section title="B. Current Experience">
To help you follow the context above, here's a look at the current Local Transfer experience and UI before any of this was optimized.
</Section>

<Section title="C. The Friction Points">
After tracing through the batch flow that people were forced to use for single transfers, the friction wasn't coming from one spot. **There were three friction points spread across three different stages of the same flow** — and all three had to be cleared in order, even for something as simple as sending to one person.

**1. Name the batch first, then you can transfer.**

Before doing anything else, users had to name their batch — something like *"Transfer for A"*. For most people who just wanted to send to one person, this was a first step that meant nothing to them.
</Section>

<ImageFull indent src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/friction-1.png" alt="'Add Batch Name' screen that had to be completed before transferring" caption="Friction #1 — Name the batch first, then you can transfer." />

<Section title=" " indent>
**2. No form — just a table and Excel upload.**

There were only two options: upload an Excel file or fill in an empty table. Both were built to handle dozens or hundreds of recipients at once. Anyone who wanted to send to one person had no other way in — they had to pick one of the two anyway.
</Section>

<ImageFull indent src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/friction-2.png" alt="Screen with two options: Upload Excel or Empty Table" caption="Friction #2 — No form. Just a table and Excel upload." />

<Section title=" " indent>
**3. Validation built for 100 people, used for 1.**

After entering the data, users still had to go through a table-based validation screen — designed to review hundreds of rows at once. For someone with a single recipient, this was a final step that was completely irrelevant, but couldn't be skipped.
</Section>

<ImageFull indent src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/friction-3.png" alt="Table-based validation screen" caption="Friction #3 — Validation built for 100 people, used for 1." />

<Section title="D. Design Opportunity">
Once the three friction points were mapped out, the real challenge wasn't just removing the roadblocks. The harder part was designing a solution that actually fit the majority — without creating new problems for everyone else.

> **How do you give 72% of users a much faster path — without creating a dead end for the ones who still need batch?**

This wasn't just about bolting a new feature onto what already existed. The real challenge was designing two paths that stood on their own but still felt like one product — a simple single-transaction path for most people, without taking away the batch path that power users relied on. Get one side wrong, and the other side would suffer too.
</Section>

<PartHeader number="03" title="Design Decisions" />

<Section title="Kickoff">
I joined after Fira had wrapped up discovery — the data was already mapped out, and the problem statement was clear. My job started from ideating solutions through to detailed UI and transition logic, all the way to an interactive prototype and handoff to engineering.

Out of the three friction points we found, the solution pointed in one direction: design a new path made specifically for single transactions. A path with no batch name, no required table, and no bulk validation to skip past — just a short form that gets straight to the point: who's it for, how much are you sending. This is what we designed as Single Transfer.

After I sketched out the first alternatives, we ran a **forum with the wider team (Designers + Writers + PM)** to align on direction and decide what needed validating. The forum's output: two things we needed to test with users:

- How users experience the full *Single Transfer* journey end-to-end.
- Which entry point option communicates the choice most clearly.

I tested both with 8 internal users in usability testing sessions.
</Section>

<Section title="A. Testing with Users">
The Single Transfer flow we tested was designed to remove all three friction points we'd found earlier — no batch name, no table, no validation built for 100 people. This round of testing validated two things: whether the new flow actually felt easier in users' hands, and which entry point most clearly communicated the difference between the two paths.
</Section>

<Section title="A.1 Testing the Single Transfer Flow" indent>
I started the session with an end-to-end walkthrough: I walked users through the Single Transfer process from start to submit, then asked what they thought of the overall flow. This was intentionally placed first so users had full context before being asked about entry points.
</Section>

<ImageFull indent={2} src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/walkthrough-full-journey.gif" alt="Full journey walkthrough of Single Transfer" caption="Result: every user said they didn't run into any friction — the flow was validated as easy to complete." />

<Section title=" " indent={2}>
**Result → Every user** found it effortless. Nobody got stuck on naming a batch, nobody was confused by a validation table — none of the three friction points that used to slow people down showed up in this flow. The flow was validated as easy to complete.
</Section>

<Section title="A.2 User Feedback on the Entry Point" indent>
Once users understood the full journey, I asked for their thoughts on the entry point. Three alternatives were tested:
</Section>

<ImageFull indent={2} src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/entry-point-alt-1.png" alt="Entry point Alt 1: Three-option hierarchy" caption="Alt 1 — Three-option hierarchy: Single Local Transfer + Batch (Upload Excel / Empty Table)." />

<ImageFull indent={2} src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/entry-point-alt-2.png" alt="Entry point Alt 2: Two-option toggle" caption="Alt 2 — Two-option toggle: Single vs Batch only." />

<ImageFull indent={2} src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/entry-point-alt-3.png" alt="Entry point Alt 3: Direct list" caption="Alt 3 — Direct list. We were tempted by this one: if 72% of transactions are single anyway, why not skip the choice entirely and just show the list?" />

<Section title="Testing Results" indent={2}>
Alt 1 and Alt 2 got the same number of user votes, but based on a pros/cons breakdown, **Alt 2 won** — clearer, and with the fewest cons. Alt 3 got zero votes — users who needed batch couldn't find their way to it.
</Section>

<Section title="B. Bringing the Results to the CEO">
Fira and I presented both sets of testing results to **Bang Edo (our CEO)**. Two key findings we highlighted:

1. The Single Transfer flow was validated as easy to complete — no user ran into friction.
2. The clearest entry point option was Alt 2 (just two buttons).

Alongside that, we pitched the direction we were leaning toward: **going more aggressively single-only**, based on the 72% data point plus both testing findings pointing the same way.

The reaction wasn't what we expected. Bang Edo raised a perspective the testing hadn't captured — not about how often people switch between single and batch, but about what happens when that switch isn't possible at all.
</Section>

<Quote indent author="Bang Edo, CEO">
This feature can handle single transactions through its own dedicated flow, but users should still be able to switch over and do a batch transaction too.
</Quote>

<Section title=" " indent>
The testing confirmed that users rarely need to switch from single to batch — but it never tested what happens when that need shows up mid-flow. That's the risk Bang Edo saw that we'd missed: a rigid Quick Transfer could become a *dead end* at the exact moment it mattered most.

This is where the feature got its name: **Quick Transfer** — not *"Single"*, because *"Single"* closes a door that should stay open. **Framing the feature turned out to matter just as much as the flow itself.**
</Section>

<Section title="C. Iterating After Bang Edo's Feedback">
After Bang Edo's pivot, I made two fundamental changes. First, the testing recommendation (Alt 2) no longer applied — the feature now needed to show all three methods at once for the transition logic to work. Second, I opened up **two entry points into batch from inside the Quick Transfer flow** — so users who suddenly needed to switch wouldn't hit a dead end.

Together, these two changes made Quick Transfer **genuinely** flexible — not just single transfer locked into one path.
</Section>

<Section title="C.1 Entry Point: Alt 2 → Alt 1 with Grouping" indent>
**The testing recommendation (Alt 2) no longer applied.** After the pivot, the feature needed to show all three methods (Quick + Upload Excel + Empty Table) at once for the transition logic to work. The structure ended up using **Alt 1**, but I handled the *"too many choices"* con with descriptive grouping: *"Suitable for 1-5 recipients"* for Quick Transfer, *"For up to 1000 recipients"* for the two batch options.
</Section>

<Section title="Recap: Three Final Design Decisions">
Out of this whole process — testing, Bang Edo's feedback, and the iteration that followed — three final design decisions took shape:

1. **Entry point Alt 1 with descriptive grouping** — communicates what each path can actually handle, not just a label.
2. **Multi-select in Recipient Details** — the first door into batch, for users who know from the start they need to send to more than one person.
3. **CTA banner in Transfer Details** — the second door, with data preservation, for users who realize mid-flow that they need batch.

Together, these three make Quick Transfer fast for the majority, without shutting the door on anyone who still needs batch.
</Section>

<PartHeader number="04" title="Solution" />

<Section title="Quick Transfer Flow">
The 72% of users who used to be stuck going through the batch flow — now have their own path. No batch name. No table. No validation process built for 100 people.

**How do you want to send money?** Now, when someone wants to make a transaction, there's an answer ready: **Quick Transfer**. Type the bank name, pick it from the dropdown, enter the account number → step 2, fill in the amount and transfer note → submit. Done. Two steps, a short form — none of the three friction points that used to slow them down.
</Section>

<Section title="Reflection">
What satisfied me most wasn't the 52% or 41% by themselves — it was the **reason** those numbers got that high.

**Bang Edo's feedback turned out to be the deciding factor.** If this feature had stayed a rigid *"Single Transfer,"* users who suddenly needed to add more recipients would have bounced back to the old batch flow, gotten frustrated, and maybe never come back to Quick Transfer next time. **It's the transition logic that made Quick Transfer something people actually use, not just something they try once.**
</Section>
