<PartHeader number="01" title="Project Snapshot" />

<Section title="Background">
Jack Finance is a business financial services provider offering integrated financial management solutions. One of its main services is ***Local Transfer***, built to help companies manage domestic payments in one place.

Since its main audience is corporate clients with high-volume transaction needs, **Local Transfer was designed from day one to handle bulk payments (*batch processing*)**.

This project and [Local Transfer Mobile](/work/local-transfer-mobile/) grew out of the same discovery. Mobile answered it on the phone first; this project answered it in the Portal. Both follow one principle: **single first, batch as a branch**.
</Section>

<PartHeader number="02" title="Context & Problem" />

<Section title="A. Context Problem">
Fira (my PM) came to me with one data point:

> **72% of batch transfers on Jack Portal only had one recipient.**

People didn't choose batch for those transfers; there was no other option. Sending to one person still meant a flow built for hundreds: name the batch, pick an upload method, work through a validation table.
</Section>

<Section title="B. Current Experience">
Sending to one person meant going through the full batch flow, from the Create menu to bulk validation.
</Section>

<ImageFull indent src="/images/cs-local-transfer-mobile/current-experience-portal.png" alt="Jack Portal dashboard with the Create menu open and Local Transfer highlighted" caption="Current experience: Local Transfer starts from the Create menu in the Portal." />

<FigmaEmbed indent url="https://www.figma.com/proto/IkEPs072TqfKuAmfF4SEbd/Jack---Local-Transfer?node-id=1875-44005&viewport=-990%2C1194%2C0.11&t=sm20zFSFZdtt8HdA-1&scaling=scale-down&content-scaling=fixed&starting-point-node-id=1875%3A43955&page-id=411%3A2&show-proto-sidebar=1" title="Current experience: Local Transfer" caption="Prototype of the old Local Transfer flow, from batch name to validation." />

<Section title="C. The Friction Points">
Fira and I traced that flow and found **three friction points at three different stages**. All three had to be cleared, even to send to one person.

**1. Name the batch first, then you can transfer.**

Before anything else, users had to name their batch, something like *"Transfer for A"*. For someone sending to one person, this step meant nothing.
</Section>

<ImageFull indent src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/friction-1.png" alt="'Add Batch Name' screen that had to be completed before transferring" caption="The Add Batch Name screen before transferring." />

<Section title=" " indent>
**2. No form, just a table and Excel upload.**

The only options were uploading an Excel file or filling in an empty table, both built for dozens or hundreds of recipients.
</Section>

<ImageFull indent src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/friction-2.png" alt="Screen with two options: Upload Excel or Empty Table" caption="The choice of Upload Excel or Empty Table." />

<Section title=" " indent>
**3. Validation built for 100 people, used for 1.**

Users then had to get through a validation table designed to review hundreds of rows. For one recipient it was irrelevant, and it couldn't be skipped.
</Section>

<ImageFull indent src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/friction-3.png" alt="Table-based validation screen" caption="A validation table built for hundreds of rows." />

<Section title="D. Design Opportunity">
Removing the three roadblocks was the easy part. The harder part was fitting the majority without creating new problems for everyone else.

> **How do you give 72% of users a much faster path, without creating a dead end for the ones who still need batch?**
</Section>

<PartHeader number="03" title="Design Decisions" />

<Section title="A. Kickoff">
I joined after Fira had wrapped up discovery, with the data mapped out and the problem statement clear. My job ran from ideating solutions to detailed UI and transition logic, an interactive prototype, and handoff to engineering.

The three friction points pointed to one answer: a separate path for single transactions, just a short form asking who it's for and how much. We called it Single Transfer.
</Section>

<Section title="B. Exploration & Validation">
After I sketched the first alternatives, we ran a **forum with the wider team (Designers + Writers + PM)** to align on direction and decide what needed validating. The forum agreed on two things to test with users:

- How users experience the full *Single Transfer* journey end-to-end.
- Which entry point option communicates the choice most clearly.

I tested both with 8 internal users in usability testing sessions: 3 from Ops, 1 from Finance and 4 from Marketing. Seven of them already used Local Transfer in the Portal; one didn't.
</Section>

<Section title="B.1 Test: The Single Transfer Flow (Concept Feedback)" indent>
Each session opened with a walkthrough of Single Transfer from start to submit, so users had full context before I asked about entry points.
</Section>

<ImageFull indent={2} src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/walkthrough-full-journey.gif" alt="Full journey walkthrough of Single Transfer" caption="The Single Transfer journey shown to participants in the walkthrough." />

<Section title=" " indent={2}>
**Every participant** said the flow felt easy to follow. None of them raised the batch name or the validation table as a concern. Because I guided them through the flow, this is concept feedback: it shows the direction made sense to users, not that they could complete it on their own.
</Section>

<Section title="B.2 Test: The Entry Point" indent>
Once users understood the full journey, I asked for their thoughts on the entry point. Three alternatives were tested:
</Section>

<ImageFull indent={2} src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/entry-point-alt-1.png" alt="Entry point Alt 1: Three-option hierarchy" caption="Alt 1: three-option hierarchy, Single Local Transfer + Batch (Upload Excel / Empty Table)." />

<ImageFull indent={2} src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/entry-point-alt-2.png" alt="Entry point Alt 2: Two-option toggle" caption="Alt 2: two-option toggle, Single vs Batch only." />

<ImageFull indent={2} src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/entry-point-alt-3.png" alt="Entry point Alt 3: Direct list" caption="Alt 3: direct list. We were tempted by this one. If 72% of transactions are single anyway, why not skip the choice entirely and just show the list?" />

<Section title=" " indent={2}>
Alt 1 and Alt 2 tied at 4 votes each. Here's what participants said about each:

| | Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alt 1: three-option hierarchy | The options help users decide which input method to use. Familiar to users who already know the Portal. | Less clear than the alternatives. Too many choices. Some components distract, so users need time to take in the options. |
| Alt 2: two-option toggle | Cleaner, with just enough information. Easier to tell the transfer methods apart. | Takes some getting used to, since input methods aren't offered up front. |
| Alt 3: direct list | None. No participant picked it. | Layout too different from the Portal users know. Batch methods aren't shown up front. |

A few reactions behind those pros and cons:

- **Alt 1:** a Marketing participant noticed the cards and their icons first, and only then worked out which option was single and which was batch.
- **Alt 2:** a Finance participant found it more appealing. The other options felt full of text, and with only two choices there was nothing to get confused about.
- **Alt 3:** an Ops participant was caught off guard by it, and pointed out that not every user thinks two steps ahead; some aren't comfortable with technology.

Weighing these, **Alt 2 won**: it was the clearest and had the fewest cons.
</Section>

<Section title="C. CEO Review">
Fira and I presented the findings to **Edo, our CEO**: the flow was easy to follow, and Alt 2 was the clearest entry point. Based on that and the 72%, we pitched **going more aggressively single-only**.

Edo raised what the testing hadn't captured: what happens when a user needs batch and can't switch, however rarely it happens.
</Section>

<Quote indent author="Edo, CEO">
This feature can handle single transactions through its own dedicated flow, but users should still be able to switch over and do a batch transaction too.
</Quote>

<Section title=" " indent>
Our sessions never tested the need for batch showing up mid-flow. A rigid single-only flow could become a *dead end* at the exact moment it mattered most.

This is where the feature got its name: **Quick Transfer**. *"Single"* closes a door that should stay open. **Framing the feature turned out to matter just as much as the flow itself.**
</Section>

<Section title="D. Final Decisions">
The flow already solved the three friction points. The test results and this review then shaped three design decisions:

- **D.1 Entry point** (picking the wrong path up front): settled by user testing and the CEO review.
- **D.2 Recipient Details** (a dead end for users who need several recipients from the start): settled after the CEO review.
- **D.3 Transfer Details** (a dead end for users who need batch mid-flow): settled after the CEO review.
</Section>

<Section title="D.1 Entry Point: From a Toggle to Grouped Options" indent>
**Problem addressed:** a fast path that only works for one transaction, and picking the wrong path up front.

Alt 2 no longer fit. Its *Single* vs *Batch* toggle tells users the fast path is for exactly one transfer, while Edo wanted a fast path that still works for a few recipients.

Half the participants had already leaned this way. All four who voted for Alt 1 gave the same reason: every option was visible up front. Edo's concern gave that preference a reason to win.

So the structure moved to **Alt 1**, with all three methods (Quick + Upload Excel + Empty Table) side by side, grouped by how much work the user has instead of *one vs many*. To handle Alt 1's *"too many choices"* con, each group states its capacity: *"Suitable for 1-5 recipients"* for Quick Transfer, *"For up to 1000 recipients"* for the two batch options.
</Section>

<Section title="D.2 Recipient Details: From One Recipient to Multi-select" indent>
**Problem addressed:** a dead end for users who know from the start that they need several recipients.

The first door into batch. I added a checkbox next to each recipient and an *"X recipients selected"* footer with a Continue button, which opens Empty Table with the selected recipients already filled in.

Testing had hinted at this too. One participant asked for a select-all button to send to every saved recipient, and an Ops participant noted that clients usually send to the same recipients again and again.
</Section>

<ImageGrid cols={1} indent={2}>

  <ImageFull src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/recipient-details-after.png" alt="After: Recipient Details with multi-select" caption="After: with multi-select" />
</ImageGrid>

<Section title="D.3 Transfer Details: From a Dead End to Adding Recipients" indent>
**Problem addressed:** a dead end, and lost data, for users who realise mid-flow that they need batch.

The second door, with data preservation. A banner below the form reads *"Click here to add more recipients"*. One click, and everything already filled in carries over to Empty Table.

The banner copy started as a question, *"Want to add more recipients?"*. With the UX writer, we changed it to a line that starts with an action, so the banner says what a click does.
</Section>

<PartHeader number="04" title="Solution" />

<Section title="A. Quick Transfer Flow">
**How do you want to send money?** When someone starts a transaction, the answer is ready: **Quick Transfer**. Pick the bank, enter the account number → step 2, fill in the amount and transfer note → submit.

<PartHeader number="05" title="Impact" />

<Section title="A. Results">
<Metrics items={[
  { value: "52%", label: "Shorter average time to start a transfer to a single recipient" },
  { value: "41%", label: "Of all Local Transfer initiations went through Quick Transfer, within 2 weeks of launch" },
]} />

Within two weeks, about 4 in 10 Local Transfers started through Quick Transfer: once users with one or a few recipients had a short path, they took it.

Qualitative feedback said the new options were much clearer, and Quick Transfer felt faster and more intuitive for users' most common task.

*Source: metrics and feedback as reported in [Quick Transfer: Boosting Portal Payments](https://app.notion.com/p/Quick-Transfer-Boosting-Portal-Payments-1e2407ef883f8088b0fcf433352e3d7b), the product portfolio of Fira Zahra Allyah, Product Manager.*
</Section>

<Section title="B. Reflection">
What satisfied me most was the **reason** behind the 52% and 41%.

**Edo's feedback turned out to be the deciding factor.** If this feature had stayed a rigid *"Single Transfer,"* users who suddenly needed to add more recipients would have bounced back to the old batch flow, gotten frustrated, and maybe never come back to Quick Transfer. **The transition logic is what made Quick Transfer something people keep using after the first try.**
</Section>
