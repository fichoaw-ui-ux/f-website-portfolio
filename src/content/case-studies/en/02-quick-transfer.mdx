<PartHeader number="01" title="Project Snapshot" />

<Section title="Background">
Jack Finance is a business financial services provider offering integrated financial management solutions. One of its main services is ***Local Transfer***, built to help companies manage domestic payments in one place.

Since its main audience is corporate clients with high-volume transaction needs, **Local Transfer was designed from day one to handle bulk payments (*batch processing*)**.

This project and [Local Transfer Mobile](/work/local-transfer-mobile/) grew out of the same discovery: 72% of Local Transfer batches held a single transaction. Local Transfer Mobile came first and answered it on the phone; this project followed and answered it in the Portal. Both follow the same principle: **single first, batch as a branch**.
</Section>

<PartHeader number="02" title="Context & Problem" />

<Section title="A. Context Problem">
Fira (my PM) came to me with one data point:

> **72% of batch transfers on Jack Portal only had one recipient.**

People didn't choose batch for those transfers. There was simply no other option. Every time someone wanted to send to just one person, they still had to go through a flow built for hundreds of recipients: name the batch, pick an upload method, work through a validation table. For a single transaction.

When Fira and I dug deeper, we found three friction points stacked in a row in the same flow, each one slowing users down before they even got to the transfer itself.
</Section>

Sending to one person meant going through the full batch flow, from the Create menu to bulk validation. The screenshot and prototype below show that journey.
</Section>

<Section title="C. The Friction Points">
Tracing the batch flow that people were forced to use for single transfers, **we found three friction points spread across three different stages of the same flow**. All three had to be cleared in order, even for something as simple as sending to one person.

**1. Name the batch first, then you can transfer.**

Before doing anything else, users had to name their batch, something like *"Transfer for A"*. For most people who just wanted to send to one person, this first step meant nothing to them.
</Section>

<ImageFull indent src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/friction-1.png" alt="'Add Batch Name' screen that had to be completed before transferring" caption="Friction #1: name the batch first, then you can transfer." />

<Section title=" " indent>
**2. No form, just a table and Excel upload.**

There were only two options: upload an Excel file or fill in an empty table. Both were built to handle dozens or hundreds of recipients at once. Anyone who wanted to send to one person had to pick one of the two anyway.
</Section>

<ImageFull indent src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/friction-2.png" alt="Screen with two options: Upload Excel or Empty Table" caption="Friction #2: no form, just a table and Excel upload." />

<Section title=" " indent>
**3. Validation built for 100 people, used for 1.**

After entering the data, users still had to go through a table-based validation screen designed to review hundreds of rows at once. For someone with a single recipient, this final step was irrelevant, and it couldn't be skipped.
</Section>

<ImageFull indent src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/friction-3.png" alt="Table-based validation screen" caption="Friction #3: validation built for 100 people, used for 1." />

<Section title="D. Design Opportunity">
Removing the three roadblocks was the easy part. The harder part was designing a solution that fit the majority without creating new problems for everyone else.

> **How do you give 72% of users a much faster path, without creating a dead end for the ones who still need batch?**

That meant designing two paths that stood on their own but still felt like one product: a simple single-transaction path for most people, next to the batch path that power users relied on. Get one side wrong, and the other side would suffer too.
</Section>

<PartHeader number="03" title="Design Decisions" />

<Section title="Kickoff">
I joined after Fira had wrapped up discovery, with the data mapped out and the problem statement clear. My job ran from ideating solutions to detailed UI and transition logic, an interactive prototype, and handoff to engineering.

The three friction points pointed in one direction: a new path made specifically for single transactions. No batch name, no required table, no bulk validation to skip past. Just a short form that asks who it's for and how much. We called it Single Transfer.

After I sketched the first alternatives, we ran a **forum with the wider team (Designers + Writers + PM)** to align on direction and decide what needed validating. The forum agreed on two things to test with users:

- How users experience the full *Single Transfer* journey end-to-end.
- Which entry point option communicates the choice most clearly.

I tested both with 8 internal users in usability testing sessions.
</Section>

<Section title="Exploration & Validation">
The Single Transfer flow we tested had none of the three friction points: no batch name, no table, no validation built for 100 people. The sessions checked two things: how the new flow came across to users, and which entry point most clearly communicated the difference between the two paths.
</Section>

<Section title="Concept Feedback: The Single Transfer Flow" indent>
I started each session with an end-to-end walkthrough: I walked users through the Single Transfer process from start to submit, then asked what they thought of the overall flow. This came first so users had full context before being asked about entry points.
</Section>

<ImageFull indent src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/walkthrough-full-journey.gif" alt="Full journey walkthrough of Single Transfer" caption="The Single Transfer journey shown to participants in the walkthrough." />

<Section title=" " indent>
**Every participant** said the flow felt easy to follow. None of them raised the batch name or the validation table as a concern. Because I guided them through the flow, this is concept feedback: it shows the direction made sense to users, not that they could complete it on their own.
</Section>

<Section title="Testing: The Entry Point" indent>
Once users understood the full journey, I asked for their thoughts on the entry point. Three alternatives were tested:
</Section>

<ImageFull indent src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/entry-point-alt-1.png" alt="Entry point Alt 1: Three-option hierarchy" caption="Alt 1: three-option hierarchy, Single Local Transfer + Batch (Upload Excel / Empty Table)." />

<ImageFull indent src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/entry-point-alt-2.png" alt="Entry point Alt 2: Two-option toggle" caption="Alt 2: two-option toggle, Single vs Batch only." />

<ImageFull indent src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/entry-point-alt-3.png" alt="Entry point Alt 3: Direct list" caption="Alt 3: direct list. We were tempted by this one. If 72% of transactions are single anyway, why not skip the choice entirely and just show the list?" />

<Section title=" " indent>
Alt 1 and Alt 2 got the same number of user votes. Here's what participants said about each:

| | Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alt 1: three-option hierarchy | The options help users decide which input method to use. Familiar to users who already know the Portal. | Less clear than the alternatives. Too many choices. Some components distract, so users need time to take in the options. |
| Alt 2: two-option toggle | Cleaner, with just enough information. Easier to tell the transfer methods apart. | Takes some getting used to, since input methods aren't offered up front. |
| Alt 3: direct list | None. No participant picked it. | Layout too different from the Portal users know. Batch methods aren't shown up front. |

Weighing these, **Alt 2 won**: it was the clearest and had the fewest cons. Alt 3 got zero votes; participants felt it couldn't work for the Portal, mostly because batch wasn't visible from the start.
</Section>

<Section title="CEO Review: No Dead Ends" indent>
Fira and I presented both sets of findings to **Edo, our CEO**. We highlighted two:

1. Participants found the Single Transfer flow easy to follow.
2. The clearest entry point option was Alt 2 (just two buttons).

Alongside that, we pitched the direction we were leaning toward: **going more aggressively single-only**, based on the 72% data point plus both findings pointing the same way.

The reaction wasn't what we expected. Edo raised a perspective the testing hadn't captured. His concern was what happens when the switch from single to batch isn't possible at all, however rarely it's needed.
</Section>

<Quote indent author="Edo, CEO">
This feature can handle single transactions through its own dedicated flow, but users should still be able to switch over and do a batch transaction too.
</Quote>

<Section title=" " indent>
Our sessions never tested what happens when the need for batch shows up mid-flow. That's the risk Edo saw and we'd missed: a rigid single-only flow could become a *dead end* at the exact moment it mattered most.

This is where the feature got its name: **Quick Transfer**. *"Single"* closes a door that should stay open. **Framing the feature turned out to matter just as much as the flow itself.**
</Section>

<Section title=" ">
At this point the three friction points were already solved, together, by the Single Transfer flow itself, and testing had pointed to Alt 2 for the entry point. What was left was the risk Edo raised: a dead end for users who need batch. The three final decisions below each answer that risk.
</Section>

<Section title="A. Entry Point: Alt 2 → Alt 1 with Grouping">
**Risk addressed:** a path that's only for one transaction, and picking the wrong path up front.

The testing recommendation (Alt 2) no longer applied. Its toggle split the choice into *Single* vs *Batch*, which tells users the fast path is for exactly one transfer. Edo wanted the opposite: a fast path that still works when a user has a few recipients.

So the structure moved to **Alt 1**, with all three methods (Quick + Upload Excel + Empty Table) side by side. The options are now grouped by how much work the user has, instead of *one vs many*. I handled Alt 1's *"too many choices"* con with descriptive grouping: *"Suitable for 1-5 recipients"* for Quick Transfer, *"For up to 1000 recipients"* for the two batch options, which marks them as the path for larger bulk payments. Users can pick the right path from the first screen.
</Section>

<Section title="B. Multi-select in Recipient Details">
**Risk addressed:** a dead end for users who know from the start that they need several recipients.

This is the first door into batch from inside the Quick Transfer flow. I added multi-select: a checkbox column next to each recipient, plus an *"X recipients selected"* indicator in the footer with a Continue button. Continue takes them to Empty Table with the selected recipients already in place, where they fill in the amount, email and notes for each one. Users who knew from the start they wanted to send to several people could select them all here, without starting over.
</Section>

<Section title="C. CTA Banner in Transfer Details">
**Risk addressed:** a dead end, and lost data, for users who realise mid-flow that they need batch.

This is the second door, with data preservation. I added a black CTA banner reading *"Click here to add more recipients"* below the form. A user who has already filled in 1 recipient plus an amount, then realises they need to send to a few more people, clicks once and everything they've entered carries over to Empty Table.
</Section>

<Section title="Recap: Three Final Design Decisions">
Out of testing, Edo's feedback, and the iteration that followed, three final design decisions took shape:

1. **Entry point Alt 1 with descriptive grouping.** Each option says what it can handle, beyond a plain label.
2. **Multi-select in Recipient Details.** The first door into batch, for users who know from the start they need to send to more than one person.
3. **CTA banner in Transfer Details.** The second door, with data preservation, for users who realise mid-flow that they need batch.

Together, these three keep Quick Transfer fast for the majority while leaving batch open to anyone who needs it.
</Section>

<PartHeader number="04" title="Solution" />

<Section title="Quick Transfer Flow">
The 72% of users who used to be stuck in the batch flow now have their own path. No batch name. No table. No validation process built for 100 people.

**How do you want to send money?** When someone starts a transaction, the answer is ready: **Quick Transfer**. Type the bank name, pick it from the dropdown, enter the account number → step 2, fill in the amount and transfer note → submit. Two steps and a short form, with none of the three friction points.
</Section>

<Section title="Two Doors Into Batch">
With the friction for the 72% solved, what's left is the gap Edo pointed out. Two entry points into batch from inside the Quick Transfer flow keep the path open for anyone who suddenly needs more.
</Section>

<Section title="Reflection">
What satisfied me most was the **reason** behind the 52% and 41%.

**Edo's feedback turned out to be the deciding factor.** If this feature had stayed a rigid *"Single Transfer,"* users who suddenly needed to add more recipients would have bounced back to the old batch flow, gotten frustrated, and maybe never come back to Quick Transfer. **The transition logic is what made Quick Transfer something people keep using after the first try.**
</Section>
