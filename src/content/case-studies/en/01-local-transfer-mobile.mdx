<PartHeader number="01" title="Project Snapshot" />

<Section title="Background">
Jack Finance is a business financial services provider offering integrated financial management solutions. One of its main services is ***Local Transfer***, built to help companies manage domestic payments in one place.

Jack runs on two platforms: **Jack Portal (web)** and the **Jack Mobile App**. Their roles weren't balanced: every *Local Transfer* could only be created in the Portal.

Every transaction at Jack passes through three roles: the **Drafter** who creates it, the **Approver** who approves it, and the **Releaser** who releases the funds from the company balance. For *Local Transfer*, the Mobile App only served Approvers and Releasers. The Drafter, the person who starts every transaction, still had to open the Portal. The only thing a Drafter could create from their phone was a *reimbursement*.

This project and [Quick Transfer](/work/quick-transfer/) grew out of the same discovery. This one answered it on the phone first; Quick Transfer answered it in the Portal. Both follow one principle: **single first, batch as a branch**.
</Section>

<PartHeader number="02" title="Context & Problem" />

<Section title="A. Context Problem">
The first signal was low Mobile App usage. Monthly active users across every mobile module sat at just **31–50 users** a month (April–September 2023), and only **18–26** of them were clients. The rest were Transfez's own staff. In September 2023, when the Portal logged **677 Local Transfer batches**, only **26 clients** were active on the Mobile App. The reason was clear: *drafters*, the people who create transactions, had no reason to open the app.

**Fira (my PM)** then found data that made it clear who was losing out the most. From an analysis of transaction patterns from January 2023 to February 2024:

> **72% of Local Transfer batches on Jack Portal held only one transaction.**

In other words, many users, especially SME owners and founders, only needed to send one payment. To do it, they still had to sit at a laptop and go through a flow built for bulk payments.

Fira backed this up by reviewing user feedback and running short interviews with a few clients and internal stakeholders. The result was consistent: **having to use a desktop for a quick, single payment felt like a hassle**.
</Section>

<Section title="B. Current Experience">
Picture a Drafter out of the office who needs to pay a supplier that same day. They open the Jack Mobile App, but there's no button to create a new transaction. The only **Create New** button on Home sits on the *Reimbursement* card. So they wait until they can open a laptop, then go through the Portal's *Local Transfer* flow, built for many recipients, to send to one person.
</Section>

<Section title="C. The Friction Points">
Two friction points had to be solved together for the feature to get used.

**1. Local Transfer didn't exist on mobile.**

The only transaction a Drafter could create in the app was a *reimbursement*. There was no Local Transfer, and no general Create button that could lead to one.

Urgent payments had to wait for a laptop, even for business owners and finance managers who work from their phones every day.
</Section>

<ImageFull indent mobile src="/images/cs-local-transfer-mobile/friction-1-home.png" alt="Jack Mobile App Home with Create New only on the Reimbursement card" caption="Create New only on the Reimbursement card." />

<Section title=" " indent>
**2. The Portal treated every transfer as a batch.**

The Portal flow always started as a batch: a batch name, a table or file upload, then bulk validation. Yet 72% of batches held only one recipient.
</Section>

<ImageFull indent src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/friction-1.png" alt="Batch name screen in Jack Portal" caption="A batch name up front." />

<ImageFull indent src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/friction-2.png" alt="Table or Excel upload screen in Jack Portal" caption="A table or Excel upload, no form." />

<ImageFull indent src="/images/cs-quick-transfer/friction-3.png" alt="Bulk validation screen in Jack Portal" caption="Bulk validation." />

<Section title=" " indent>
On a phone, that load weighs even more. A batch-first flow can't be carried over to mobile as it is.
</Section>

<Section title="D. Design Opportunity">
Moving the feature to mobile was only half the job. The question that started the design:

> **How do you make Local Transfer feel natural on a phone for a single transfer, while keeping batch open for the people who still need it?**

A shrunk-down Portal flow would still be a hassle for the 72% of single transfers. Dropping batch would push users who occasionally pay several people back to their laptops. The goal was one flow that's fast for a single transfer and can grow into a batch without starting over.
</Section>

<PartHeader number="03" title="Design Decisions" />

<Section title="A. Kickoff">
After Fira wrapped up *discovery*, with the data mapped out and the problem statement clear, I joined as the UI/UX Designer. My work ran from exploring user flows and iterating on the UI to usability testing and handoff to mobile engineering.
</Section>

<Section title="B. Exploration & Validation">
I designed **two end-to-end flow iterations** and tested them with **8 internal Jack/Transfez users**: 5 already familiar with the Portal's Local Transfer flow, 3 not. Participants were internal staff rather than real Drafters, so the results show which flow was easiest to understand, not how Drafters behave day to day.
</Section>

<Section title="B.1 Test: The Order of Steps" indent>
The PRD originally asked for mobile transaction creation to mirror the Portal's process. The question: should the steps still follow the Portal, or follow the pattern of banking and e-wallet apps? I tested this with two iterations:

- **Iteration 1:** Pick the recipient first. Batch is decided up front with checkboxes; the number of recipients checked decides single or batch.
- **Iteration 2:** The same order as the Portal. Set the destination and transaction details first.

Try both iterations below. Each prototype opens on an intro screen; click **Go to page** to jump straight into the Local Transfer flow.
</Section>

<Section title=" " indent={2}>
| | Portal order (Iteration 2) | Banking & e-wallet pattern (Iteration 1) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single | Transfer details → Recipient | Recipient → Transfer details |
| Batch | Batch details → Recipient → Transfer details | Recipient → Transfer details → Batch details |
| Picked by | **3 users** | **5 users** |

Most users picked the pattern they already knew from banking and e-wallet apps. They were used to sending money that way, and to choosing who to pay before filling in the transfer details.

Familiarity with the Portal pulled users toward the Portal order, but not all of them. Of the 5 users who knew the Portal, 3 picked the Portal order to keep it consistent and to know what a batch is for before adding recipients. The other 2 still picked this pattern; on a phone, being asked for batch details first felt off.

From this comparison, **Iteration 1 won**: for most users, consistency with phone habits mattered more than consistency with the Portal.
</Section>

<Section title="C. CEO Review">
I presented these results to **Edo, our CEO**. Iteration 1 won on order, yet it still asked about batch up front: every transfer started with checkboxes.

Edo leaned toward opening straight on the recipient list without checkboxes, so every transfer starts as a single one. The data backed him up: 72% of batches held only one transaction.

This input touched something the test didn't answer. The test only compared the order of steps, not whether batch should be asked about up front. With checkboxes in Iteration 1, every transfer still carried a batch decision, even though most transfers go to a single recipient.

So the question shifted: not *how* to show batch, but *when* batch should appear. **The test's winner wasn't automatically the final answer.**
</Section>

<Section title="D. Final Decisions">
The team's call, the test results and this review then led to three design decisions:

- **D.1 Entry point** (Friction #1): settled as a team decision.
- **D.2 Order of steps** (Friction #1): settled by user testing.
- **D.3 The batch load** (Friction #2): settled after the CEO review.
</Section>

<Section title="D.1 Entry Point: From the Reimbursement Card to a Create Button" indent>
**Problem addressed:** Friction #1, Local Transfer didn't exist on mobile.

The first step was giving Drafters a way in. Before, creating a transaction on Home was only possible through the Reimbursement card. Now there's a **Create** button in the middle of the bottom navigation, which opens a choice of transaction types, including Local Transfer.

This was a team decision. More and more features were being built into the app, so they needed one central place to start from.
</Section>

<Section title="D.2 Order of Steps: From the Portal’s Order to “Pick the Recipient First”" indent>
**Problem addressed:** Friction #1, Local Transfer didn't exist on mobile.

An entry point alone wasn't enough. Following the test results, the mobile flow starts with picking the recipient, then filling in the transfer details, just like banking and e-wallet apps. So the mobile order no longer follows the Portal, even though the PRD had asked it to.
</Section>

<Section title="D.3 The Batch Load: From Batch-first to Single-first" indent>
**Problem addressed:** Friction #2, the Portal treated every transfer as a batch.

The Portal's batch load couldn't come along to the phone, but batch couldn't disappear either. Building on Edo's input, I designed **Iteration 3**: the same recipient-first order, with batch opening only through the add-recipient icon.
</Section>

<FigmaEmbed indent={2} url="https://www.figma.com/proto/9FaFxxDoTxNaEUkl0BDyuF/Study-Case?page-id=402%3A60&node-id=7247-5508&viewport=423%2C558%2C0.09&t=MTvGewjrtSo09Fdn-1&scaling=scale-down&content-scaling=fixed&starting-point-node-id=7247%3A5508&show-proto-sidebar=1" title="Iteration 3: batch opened through the add-recipient icon" caption="Try sending to one recipient, then add more through the add-recipient icon." />

<Section title=" " indent={2}>
**Iteration 3** became the final flow. A single transfer carries no batch steps at all, and batch is still there through the add-recipient icon when it's needed.

*Batch became a branch of the single flow instead of a separate path from the start.*
</Section>

<ImageFull indent={2} src="/images/cs-local-transfer-mobile/batch-flow.png" alt="Local Transfer mobile user flow: single by default, batch as an optional branch" caption="User flow: single by default, batch as an optional branch." />

<PartHeader number="04" title="Solution" />

<Section title="A. Local Transfer on the Phone">
Drafters who used to wait until they could open a laptop can now create a Local Transfer straight from the Jack Mobile App, with single as the default and batch one tap away.
</Section>

<Section title="B. Final Flow">
For a single transfer: open **Create** → pick **Local Transfer** → pick the recipient → enter the amount → confirm and submit. No batch name, no table.
</Section>

<FigmaEmbed indent url="https://www.figma.com/proto/9FaFxxDoTxNaEUkl0BDyuF/Study-Case?page-id=402%3A60&node-id=7193-28927&viewport=-774%2C815%2C0.16&t=SEKsuUnJn7uITSbC-1&scaling=scale-down&content-scaling=fixed&starting-point-node-id=7193%3A28927&show-proto-sidebar=1" title="Prototype: Local Transfer in the Jack Mobile App" caption="Prototype of the final Local Transfer flow on mobile." />

<Section title="C. Key Details">
- **Batch without starting over.** If users realise halfway through that they need to send to several people, they tap the **add-recipient icon** and the flow shifts to batch, keeping what they've entered.
- **Batch details at the end.** Batch details are only asked for at the end. Before submitting, users see a summary with Edit/Delete options per transaction.
- **A cap of 10 recipients.** Batch on mobile holds up to **10 recipients** (the average Portal batch holds 8 transactions) with no file upload. Large batches still go through the Portal.
</Section>

<Section title="B. Reflection">
**What I learned most from this project:** designing for mobile means rethinking the flow for the phone. Shrinking the desktop feature doesn't work. Even among users who knew the Portal, most picked the phone pattern over the Portal's order. Testing wasn't the last word either: the final flow came from a review after testing, backed by usage data.

> **Users bring habits from the other apps on their phones, and good design follows those habits.**
</Section>
